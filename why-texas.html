Climate Change in Texas

Why Texas?

Why regional climate matters, and why Texas is one of the most important places to study the present and future effects of climate change.

Source: Great Stays of Texas, “Discover the Magic of Texas Bluebonnets”

Climate change is a global issue, but its impacts are not evenly distributed. Different regions experience climate change in distinct ways depending on geography, atmospheric patterns, and environmental conditions. Texas provides a particularly important case study because of its size, geographic diversity, and exposure to extreme weather.

Why Focus on Texas?

Texas is part of the Southern Great Plains, a region with major climate contrasts and frequent weather extremes. In Regional Climate Trends and Scenarios for the U.S. National Climate Assessment: Part 4. Climate of the U.S. Great Plains, Kunkel et al. describe the area as having “a highly diverse climate with large spatial variations,” including both “some of the coldest and hottest regions of the coterminous U.S. as well as some of the wetter and drier regions.”

The report explains that the Gulf of Mexico is the main source of moisture for the region, while the lack of mountain barriers to the north leaves the area exposed to Arctic air outbreaks. This combination helps create the dramatic swings in temperature and precipitation that make Texas especially important in a project about climate change and weather extremes.

Source: Kunkel et al., 2013, pp. 11–12.

Regional Climate Patterns in the Great Plains

Kunkel et al. show that the Great Plains include an enormous climate range. The report notes that “average annual temperatures in south Texas are greater than 70°F,” while colder northern and mountain areas are far cooler. It also explains that “average annual precipitation also exhibits an extremely large range,” from less than 10 inches in some western areas to more than 60 inches in the far southeast.

Kunkel et al. (2013) “Precipitation tends to be erratic, and severe droughts occur from time to time.”

This matters for Texas because it means the state already experiences strong climate variability. When climate change shifts temperatures and rainfall patterns further, those changes can intensify problems that already exist, including drought, flooding, and severe storms.

Source: Kunkel et al., 2013, pp. 11–13.

A Region Already Experiencing Climate Change

The Fifth National Climate Assessment makes it clear that climate change is not only a future concern, but an ever present one. The assessment states that “the effects of human-caused climate change are already far-reaching and worsening across every region of the United States.” That statement is important for a Texas-focused project because it places Texas within a national pattern of measurable climate change.

The same assessment explains that “changes in multiple aspects of climate are apparent in every US region,” including warming, changing precipitation, and heavier rainfall events. That broad conclusion helps support the regional evidence shown in Texas and the Southern Great Plains.

Source: Fifth National Climate Assessment, Overview chapter.

Why Texas Is Especially Vulnerable

Texas is especially vulnerable because it already experiences many of the weather and climate extremes that are becoming more intense in a warming climate. Kunkel et al. describe the Great Plains as a region where warmer months bring “heat waves and convective storms, including thunderstorms and lightning, flood-producing rainstorms, hail, and deadly tornadoes.” The report also notes that hurricanes are “a major weather phenomenon for the coastal region of Texas.”

Kunkel et al. (2013) “This area has the highest incidence of tornadoes in the world due to the unique confluence of several geographical factors.”

The Fifth National Climate Assessment strengthens that argument by showing how significant the region is for understanding climate impacts. As Kunkel et al. (2013) explain, “the Great Plains region experiences a wide range of extreme weather and climate events that affect human society, ecosystems, and infrastructure.” Together, these sources show why Texas is not just relevant to climate change discussions, but central to them.
