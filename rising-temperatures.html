Climate Change in Texas

Rising Temperatures

Texas has warmed over the past several decades, and that warming is making extreme heat a more serious part of the state’s climate.

Source: Facebook

Climate change in Texas is visible in the temperature record. Long-term data shows that average annual temperatures have risen over time, and scientific studies explain why this warming matters for heatwaves, health, energy demand, wildfire conditions, and agriculture.

Texas has warmed over time

The most direct evidence on this page comes from NOAA's nClimDiv dataset. By averaging the temperature data into one statewide annual value for each year, it becomes easier to see how Texas' temperatures have changed over time.

Even with year to year fluctuations, the long-term direction of the graph is upward. More recent years are generally warmer than earlier ones, which is exactly the kind of pattern expected in a warming climate.

Nielsen-Gammon (2024) “Since 1950, the trend has been 0.29 °F per decade, and since 1975, 0.62 °F per decade. Recent temperatures have increased in all seasons and in all regions of Texas.”

This quote is important because it confirms that the warming shown in the graph is not limited to a single region or season. It is a statewide trend.

Sources: Nielsen-Gammon (2024), pp. 3; NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) (2026).

How to read the trend

A climate graph does not need to rise every single year to show warming. What matters is the larger pattern across many decades. In Texas, annual temperatures still vary from year to year, but the later part of the record is clearly warmer than the earlier part.

That means the graph is showing more than isolated hot years. It is showing a long-term shift in baseline climate conditions. When average temperatures rise, extreme heat becomes easier to reach and sustain.

Nielsen-Gammon (2024) “If recent trends continue, as expected, a middle-of-the-road estimate of the overall rate of temperature increase in Texas would be about 0.6 °F per decade.”

Nielsen-Gammon's projection makes the trend more concrete: if the recent rate continues, a typical Texas year in the near future will be warmer than almost all years in the historical record.

Sources: Nielsen-Gammon (2024), pp. 3; NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) (2026).

Rising averages lead to more extreme heat

Average warming matters because it changes the conditions that produce heatwaves. When the baseline climate gets hotter, dangerous heat becomes more common and lasts longer.

Perkins-Kirkpatrick & Gibson (2017) “As anthropogenic influence on the global climate intensifies, future increases in heatwaves are unavoidable.”

Rising average temperatures are not just increasing overall warmth; they are making extreme heat more frequent and more persistent. As shown in the graph “Average Annual Number of 100°F Days,” hotter baseline conditions mean that Texas is experiencing more days of extreme heat than in the past. This aligns with broader climate research, which finds that “future increases in heatwaves are unavoidable” as human influence on the climate grows. Together, the data and research show that warming is not just gradual. It is actively increasing the frequency and severity of extreme heat across Texas.

Sources: Nielsen-Gammon (2024), pp. 6; Perkins-Kirkpatrick & Gibson (2017), Changes in Regional Heatwave Characteristics, pp. 5.

Why this matters in Texas

Rising temperatures affect more than climate statistics. In Texas, they shape public health risks, energy use, wildfire conditions, and agricultural stress.

Health

Hotter conditions increase the risk of heat exhaustion, dehydration, and heat-related illness, especially during extended summer heat events.

Energy Demand

Higher temperatures increase air-conditioning use and place additional strain on the electric grid during peak summer demand.

Wildfire Conditions

Rising heat can dry vegetation and increase evaporation, creating conditions that are more favorable for wildfire in several parts of Texas.

Agriculture

Heat stress affects crops, livestock, and soil moisture, making agricultural production and water management more difficult.

In other words, rising temperatures are not just a scientific abstraction. They affect how Texans live, work, and prepare for the future.

Sources: Nielsen-Gammon (2024); Perkins-Kirkpatrick & Gibson (2017).
