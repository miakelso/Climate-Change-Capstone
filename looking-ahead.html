Climate Change in Texas

Looking Ahead: Risks and Responses

The trends shown throughout this project point in the same direction: Texas is becoming hotter, rainfall is becoming less predictable, and drought and extreme events are becoming more consequential. The future is not fixed, but the risks are increasing.

Source: StockCake

Climate change in Texas is not just about isolated numbers on separate graphs. Rising temperatures, shifting rainfall patterns, and increasing drought stress combine to raise the likelihood of damaging extreme events. At the same time, climate projections show that the choices made today still matter for how severe those impacts become.

From trends to extreme events

Diffenbaugh et al. (2017) “Historical warming has increased the severity and probability of the hottest month and hottest day of the year at >80% of the available observational area.”

Climate change does not cause every individual storm or heatwave by itself. But it does increase risk. Warmer background conditions make extreme heat more likely, heavier rainfall more intense, and drought conditions more damaging when they occur.

That is why the trends shown across Texas matter. Rising temperatures increase the chance of dangerous heat. Changing rainfall patterns increase the chance of both heavy downpours and prolonged dryness. In combination, these shifts make extreme events more likely and more severe rather than simply changing average conditions.

Perkins-Kirkpatrick & Gibson (2017) “As anthropogenic influence on the global climate intensifies, future increases in heatwaves are unavoidable. Some regions where intense heat is already common may become inhabitable, while tropical regions will experience extremely large increases in heatwave frequency due to low interannual variability.”

Sources: Diffenbaugh et al. (2017); Perkins-Kirkpatrick & Gibson (2017).

Where is Texas headed?

Climate models project that the trends already visible in Texas will continue and intensify. The goal of projections is not to predict the exact weather in a single year. Instead, they estimate the broader direction of risk under continued warming.

Nielsen-Gammon (2024) “The number of 100-degree days at typical stations is expected to quadruple by 2036 compared to the 1970s and 1980s.”

Texas-specific projections point toward more heat, greater drought stress, and heavier extreme rainfall. Nielsen-Gammon’s 2024 update projects warmer average conditions, many more 100°F days, and stronger extreme precipitation in Texas over the coming decades.

More heat

Average temperatures are expected to keep rising, and extreme heat days are projected to become much more common.

More intense rainfall

Extreme precipitation is expected to increase, even though total rainfall can remain highly variable from place to place and year to year.

Greater drought risk

Higher temperatures and evaporative demand increase the chance that dry periods turn into more severe water stress.

More climate variability

Some of the biggest future changes come not only from shifting averages, but from more extreme swings between wet and dry conditions.

Swain & Hayhoe (2015) “Variability is also projected to increase ... indicating that climate may become more extreme under greater warming.”

Sources: Nielsen-Gammon (2024); NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) (2026); Swain & Hayhoe (2015); U.S. Global Change Research Program (2023).

What this means for Texas

These projected changes are not abstract. They affect how cities, water systems, agriculture, and everyday life in Texas will function in the future.

Cities & Infrastructure

More intense rainfall and higher temperatures put stress on roads, drainage systems, power demand, and urban flood risk.

Water Resources

Greater drought risk and higher evaporation make long-term water planning more difficult for reservoirs, utilities, and communities.

Health & Safety

Extreme heat raises health risks, especially for people who are outdoors, lack cooling, or already face social and economic vulnerability.

Landscapes & Fire Risk

Hotter conditions and prolonged dryness can make wildfire conditions more favorable in parts of Texas, especially where fuels dry out more quickly.

Fifth National Climate Assessment (2023) “The effects of human-caused climate change are already far-reaching and worsening across every region of the United States.”

In Texas, that means the future is not only warmer. It is also more demanding for systems that depend on reliable water, manageable heat, and resilient infrastructure.

Source: U.S. Global Change Research Program (2023), Fifth National Climate Assessment.

What can be done

Climate change can still be slowed, and many of its impacts can be managed. A strong response requires both mitigation and adaptation.

Fifth National Climate Assessment (2023) “Rapidly reducing greenhouse gas emissions can limit future warming and associated increases in many risks.”

Solutions matter because every fraction of warming avoided reduces future risk. The data throughout this project shows where Texas is headed, but it does not show a future that is beyond influence. Policy choices, infrastructure decisions, and energy transitions all shape what comes next.

Source: U.S. Global Change Research Program (2023), Fifth National Climate Assessment.

The future is still shaped by choices made today

Texas is already experiencing the effects of climate change, but those effects are not the end of the story. The evidence points toward increasing risk, yet it also shows why action now matters for what Texas looks like in the decades ahead.
