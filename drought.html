Water Stress in Texas

Drought in Texas is becoming more severe as rising temperatures and changing precipitation patterns intensify water loss and place growing pressure on communities, agriculture, and ecosystems.

Source: NBC News

Drought has always been part of Texas climate, but climate change is making it more severe and more difficult to manage. Rising temperatures increase evaporation, changing rainfall patterns lengthen dry periods, and declining soil moisture intensifies water stress across the state.

The drought threat is becoming more severe

Nielsen-Gammon et al. (2020) “Climate projections for the 21st century portray ‘unprecedented’ drought risk for the U.S. Southwest and Great Plains.”

Texas sits directly within this area of elevated drought risk. Nielsen-Gammon et al. explain that climate models robustly project drying of summer-season soil moisture across both eastern and western Texas during the 21st century. They also warn that future conditions may become drier than even the most arid centuries of the last 1,000 years.

This matters because drought in Texas is no longer only a question of below-normal rainfall. It is increasingly shaped by a warmer atmosphere, greater evaporation, and longer-lasting moisture deficits that make dry conditions harder to reverse.

Source: Nielsen-Gammon et al. (2020), pp. 1, 5-6; NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, Climate at a Glance: Statewide Time Series.

Drought over time

These visuals help show that drought is a recurring feature of Texas climate, but recent droughts have occurred in a context of rising temperatures and increasing pressure on water resources. The result is a more serious form of water stress, where dry conditions can intensify quickly and produce broader consequences.

Nielsen-Gammon et al. (2020) “The worst single year of drought across the state occurred in 2011.”

The 2011 drought is especially important because it illustrates how extreme drought can affect the entire state at once, not only environmentally but economically and socially as well.

Source: NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information; Nielsen-Gammon et al. (2020), p. 7.

Why drought is getting worse

Drought in Texas is becoming more severe because several climate factors are working together. Less available soil moisture, higher evaporation, and longer dry periods all contribute to stronger and more persistent water stress.

Less soil moisture

Climate models consistently project drying of summer-season soil moisture in Texas. This reduces the amount of water available to crops, vegetation, and surface systems.

Higher evaporation

As temperatures rise, water leaves the land more quickly. Even when rain falls, more moisture is lost back to the atmosphere through evaporation and plant water use.

Longer dry periods

Changing precipitation patterns increase the time between useful rainfall events, making it easier for drought conditions to develop and persist.

More extreme variability

Swain and Hayhoe project that climate variability itself may increase, meaning more extreme dry and wet seasons under greater warming.

Nielsen-Gammon et al. (2020) “Even if the amount of water vapor increases to keep pace with temperature, higher temperatures lead to greater evaporation rates.”

Nielsen-Gammon et al. also explain that future drying in Texas occurs from the combined influence of declining precipitation and increased evaporative demand from a warmer atmosphere. Swain and Hayhoe similarly conclude that climate variability is projected to increase, indicating more extreme conditions under continued warming. Together, these findings show that drought is being intensified by both temperature and rainfall changes.

Sources: Nielsen-Gammon et al. (2020), pp. 3-6; Swain & Hayhoe (2015), p. 1-2.

Why this matters in Texas

Water supply

Drought lowers reservoir storage, reduces streamflow, and strains groundwater supplies, making it harder to meet the needs of a growing population.

Agriculture

Crops and livestock are highly vulnerable to prolonged heat and moisture deficits, which can reduce yields and raise production costs.

Wildfires

Dry soils, stressed vegetation, and prolonged heat create conditions that make wildfire more likely and more destructive.

Economic impact

Severe drought produces major financial losses for agriculture, local communities, and water systems across the state.

Nielsen-Gammon et al. (2020) “This event left the state with 7.6 billion dollars in agricultural and livestock losses, 301 million dead trees ... and many dried-up lakes and rivers.”

This quote refers to the 2011 drought and shows how severe drought affects far more than weather statistics. It affects food production, ecosystems, local economies, and the long-term resilience of Texas water resources.

Sources: Nielsen-Gammon et al. (2020), p. 7; Swain & Hayhoe (2015).
